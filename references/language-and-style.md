# Language and style: Strunk adapted to scientific writing

Prefer precision, coherence, and economy. Apply the principles in context; they are not mechanical replacement rules. Source rule numbers below refer to the supplied Strunk PDF, whose numbering differs from some later editions. See [source map](source-map.md) for locations and historical qualifications.

## Paragraphs and information flow — rules 9–10, 18

Give each paragraph a coherent purpose that advances the section. A topic sentence near the beginning often helps readers locate that purpose, but a brief transition or necessary setup may come first. Support the proposition with evidence, explanation, or reasoning; let the ending establish a useful consequence or prepare the next step. Do not end every paragraph with a stock summary or force a one-sentence paragraph to expand without a reason.

Read topic sentences in sequence to expose missing steps, repetitions, and abrupt changes. Repair the logical relationship before adding a transition word. 'Therefore' asserts an inference; 'however' asserts a relevant contrast. Neither can supply the missing reasoning.

Usually begin from a recognizable topic and place the consequential new information where it receives emphasis, often toward the end. Do not delay a necessary qualifier, bury the real result behind a figure reference, or distort syntax merely to obtain end emphasis. Keep technical terms consistent when they refer to the same entity.

## Voice and verbs — rule 11

Prefer an explicit subject and an informative verb. Replace empty verb-noun combinations when meaning is preserved: 'performed an analysis of' can often become 'analyzed'. Keep a noun when it is a technical concept or when the noun phrase organizes the argument better.

Use active voice when the actor matters and is known. Use passive voice when the procedure or recipient is the natural topic, the actor is irrelevant, or disciplinary conventions favor it. Strunk explicitly allows passive voice. Do not add 'we' unless it clearly refers to the study's authors, or make an instrument, dataset, or model sound capable of a human judgment it did not perform.

Do not equate every form of 'be' with passive voice. 'The sample was stable' is a description; its quality depends on whether stability is defined and supported.

## Affirmative clarity and calibrated certainty — rule 12

Replace evasive negatives with direct statements only when they are logically equivalent in context. Preserve real negatives and qualifiers: 'not all', 'not detected', 'not statistically significant', and 'not necessarily' often carry the result itself.

'No evidence of an effect' is not 'evidence of no effect'. 'Did not inhibit' is not 'promoted'. 'Not significant' is not 'equivalent'. A clear negative result can be stronger writing than an unjustified positive claim.

State well-supported findings directly and attach uncertainty to the precise inference that needs it. Avoid both stacked hedges and unearned certainty. Distinguish speculation about a mechanism from uncertainty in a reported observation.

## Concision — rule 13

Delete text that adds neither meaning nor a useful reading cue. Common candidates include empty announcements, duplicated background, ornamental intensifiers, and vague nouns that merely restate the verb. Combine repetitive statements or split overloaded ones according to the thought, not a sentence-length quota.

Examples are suggestions, not global substitutions:

| Wordy expression | Possible revision | Check first |
|---|---|---|
| in order to measure | to measure | Preserve intended purpose |
| it was observed that X increased | X increased | Keep the observation boundary if it matters |
| carried out a comparison of A and B | compared A and B | Keep who compared them if relevant |
| due to the fact that | because | The causal relation must already be intended and supported |
| a large number of samples | many samples, or the actual n | Never invent the count |
| very high efficiency | the reported efficiency | Retain metric and conditions; do not fabricate a replacement |

Keep details needed for reproducibility and interpretation: comparator, unit, denominator, uncertainty, sample identity, concentration, time, environment, and exclusions where consequential. Repetition of a technical term can be necessary cohesion. Never sacrifice scientific completeness to a target percentage of word reduction.

## Rhythm and parallel structure — rules 14–15

Use sentence structures that express the actual relationship among ideas. Break chains of loosely joined claims when they obscure hierarchy; retain a longer sentence when it gives a clear conditional or comparison. Do not replace every sentence with the same subject–verb pattern.

Express matched quantities and conditions in matched grammatical forms. Compare like with like: a method with a method, or its accuracy with another method's accuracy. Align correlative pairs and list items. A list is useful for many genuinely parallel items; arbitrary synonym changes obscure the relationship.

Variation should follow meaning. Avoid regular three-part slogans, ritual 'Moreover/Furthermore' openings, ornamental contrast formulas, and obligatory concluding praise. Requests to reduce 'AI味' mean make the prose specific and natural while preserving the science, not manipulate a detector.

## Attachment and scope — rules 7, 16

Keep subjects near their verbs and modifiers near the words they modify. Give 'this', 'it', and 'which' unambiguous antecedents; 'this increase' may be better than 'this'. Untangle noun stacks when the hierarchy is unclear.

A participial opening needs the right actor. In 'Using microscopy, the particles were characterized', the particles did not use the microscope; revise to 'The particles were characterized by microscopy' or a supported author-subject version.

Place 'only', 'at least', 'approximately', and negatives where their scope is clear. Do not move them as decoration. Restrictive relative clauses identify a subset; nonrestrictive clauses add information. A comma can change which samples are being described, so punctuation can require a scientific author query.

## Tense — adapt rule 17

Maintain a consistent temporal viewpoint for the same rhetorical function. Scientific prose legitimately mixes tenses: completed procedures and observations often take past tense; established knowledge, definitions, and descriptions of what a figure shows often take present tense; accumulated research may take present perfect. A hypothesis or prospective procedure needs its appropriate modality or future sense.

Do not impose one tense across an abstract or manuscript. Preserve chronological relationships and distinguish performed work from proposed work. Follow an explicit journal convention when supplied.

## Usage, punctuation, and typography — rules 1–6, 8; chapters IV–VI

For clause-by-clause diagnosis, a disputed grammatical construction, or an explanation with examples, read [grammar and punctuation](grammar-and-punctuation.md). It expands the supplied PPT's possessives, list and parenthetical punctuation, conjunctions, sentence fragments, modifier repairs, variable mappings, and word-division guidance. Apply only the relevant part.

Correct possessives, agreement, articles, countability, and sentence boundaries. Avoid comma splices and accidental fragments; use conjunctions, semicolons, or separate sentences according to meaning. Set off parenthetical material consistently. Use a serial comma if it improves clarity or fits house style, without treating one convention as universal.

Preserve quotations and reference keys. Keep punctuation around parentheses and citations consistent with the required style. Treat journal headings, reference formatting, number style, and units according to the current applicable convention. Leave line-end hyphenation to the layout system; do not insert manual word breaks into editable prose.

Check scientific terms against the supplied terminology and discipline, not an ordinary spellcheck dictionary alone. Protect gene/protein names, mathematical notation, chemical formulas, software names, and proper nouns. Correct spelling consistently in the chosen English variety.

The book's historical word preferences are not a ban list. Do not replace a legitimate 'system', 'factor', 'feature', 'phase', or 'respectively' when it carries a defined scientific meaning. Retain 'respectively' when it unambiguously maps quantities to samples. Keep standard sentence-initial 'However' and inclusive singular 'they' when appropriate. Resolve ambiguous temporal/contrastive 'while' by clarifying meaning, not banning it.

## Final semantic comparison

Read the revised statement against the original: same entity and actor, same comparison, same direction and magnitude, same conditions, same logical and statistical qualifiers, same attribution? Then read it in context for coherence. An improvement in fluency is insufficient if any of those changed without authorization.
