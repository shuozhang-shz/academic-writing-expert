# Titles, abstracts, and keywords

Use this guide when drafting, revising, or checking these parts of a paper, especially when the request concerns discoverability or keywords. Read only the relevant section for a local edit; a title request does not require an abstract and keyword package. Retain the scientific invariants and editing depth in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). For other sections use [section guidance](manuscript-sections.md).

Contents: reader and journal constraints; title forms; an independent abstract; keyword selection; the joint reading test.

## 1. Reader and journal constraints

These components help a reader find the paper, judge its relevance, and decide whether to read further. They should communicate the actual study to readers, editors, and reviewers. Search visibility is a reason to use recognizable scientific terms, not to add fashionable but unstudied topics or promise more citations.

Use explicit user constraints and the current target journal's instructions for title length, abstract format and length, keyword count, allowed abbreviations, and placement. Follow [journal adaptation](manuscript-sections.md#journal-adaptation) when a live policy check is needed. A missing journal does not block a provisional draft.

The supplied lecture suggests roughly 10–15 title words, 150–250 abstract words, and 5–8 submitted keywords; it also suggests starting from 3–5 core concepts. These are planning examples, not universal limits or contradictory mandatory counts. Do not pad a short abstract, truncate an essential title qualifier, or add marginal keywords to reach them. Follow the actual request and genre.

## 2. Title: choose a form that matches the evidence

First identify the subject or phenomenon, the specific question or supported contribution, and the method, material, population, or condition that distinguishes this study. Not every element belongs in the title. Keep any qualifier whose removal changes the scope, and use the principal scientific search terms naturally.

| Form | Best use | Check before choosing |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive or nominal | Name the phenomenon, relation, or approach without declaring the answer | Does it identify this paper rather than only its broad field? Include a method or material if it distinguishes the work |
| Declarative or result-led | State a central finding that the study establishes | Does every causal, mechanistic, quantitative, or generality claim have support? Strong verbs are not a substitute for evidence |
| Interrogative | Frame the specific question that organizes the paper | Does the manuscript actually address it and give a bounded answer? Do not manufacture suspense or imply a yes/no conclusion the study cannot supply |

A colon or subtitle can separate the subject from a necessary method or scope qualifier; use it only if it improves comprehension and fits the journal. Mechanism language requires mechanism evidence even in an otherwise descriptive title. Words such as significant, reveals, or proves must not be imported from a teaching template.

Remove vague announcements and promotional adjectives before deleting useful content. Avoid unfamiliar acronyms and unexplained local sample codes. Retain established technical names when they are the clearest identifiers. Check grammar, punctuation, and title case against the requested style. A quick reader test is whether a colleague can identify what was studied without reading the abstract; the lecture's three-second prompt is a diagnostic, not a measured performance threshold.

## 3. Abstract: a complete account that stands alone

The existing [abstract guidance](manuscript-sections.md#abstract) supplies the evidence and scope checks. Use the following functions to diagnose missing content, without imposing sentence quotas:

- **Context and gap:** the minimum background needed to understand the unresolved problem.
- **Purpose:** the specific question, objective, or hypothesis addressed here.
- **Approach:** the design, study objects, and decisive method needed to interpret the answer.
- **Findings:** the principal supplied results, with meaningful comparisons and essential conditions.
- **Conclusion:** the answer and justified implication, including a boundary that changes its interpretation.

These functions can appear in a coherent unstructured paragraph or under required headings. An empirical-paper teaching template does not require structured headings for every journal or force experimental methods into a review or theoretical abstract. Preserve the distinction between a proposed approach, a numerical demonstration, and an experiment. Neither a p-value nor a method's description alone establishes a useful predictor or a mechanism.

Read the abstract without the main text. Replace dependencies such as “as shown in Fig. 2” with the actual supported finding. Usually omit bibliographic citations, footnotes, and figure/table references; retain an essential citation only if allowed by the target format, and do not silently strip required attribution from a supplied passage. Remove dispensable acronyms, define necessary unfamiliar ones within the abstract, and respect journal exceptions for standard abbreviations. A separate graphical abstract does not make the text abstract a place to embed an unexplained figure.

Integrate relevant key terms as part of normal scientific sentences. Do not insert a keyword list into the prose or repeat terms for a target density. Preserve functional tense choices: completed methods and observations often use past tense; context, the paper's purpose, or a supported interpretation may use present. See [tense and emphasis](composition-decisions.md#8-tense-and-information-emphasis--rules-1718) when a mixed-tense passage needs diagnosis. Recheck the final word count after revision and verify that every abstract result is in the paper.

## 4. Keywords: selection, placement, and a useful search check

Build a candidate pool from the actual research topic, central problem, method, material or population, theory, and principal phenomenon or finding. Think of the words a relevant reader would search for. Select a compact set of recognizable terms or noun phrases that covers the paper at complementary levels: the field, the specific phenomenon, and a distinctive method or object where relevant. This is not a requirement to fill one slot from every category.

Prefer an informative phrase to generic entries such as research, analysis, or study. An established methodological term such as finite element analysis is different from the isolated word analysis. Use community terminology rather than invented acronyms; retain a familiar abbreviation when appropriate to the audience and journal. Do not add a method merely because it is related to the field, or a mechanism that the paper only speculates about.

Remove redundant variants within the keyword list. Overlap with the title or abstract is not automatically a defect: central terms often belong in all three. Complement them with other relevant specific terms where space permits. Apply a prohibition on title-word repetition only when the actual journal requires it; do not create awkward synonyms to satisfy the lecture's broad warning about repetition.

Follow the journal's count, ordering, capitalization, separator, and submission-field rules. In a provisional manuscript, a separate `Keywords: term; term; term` line below the abstract is a useful presentation convention. The final keyword list and the submission form should agree; preparing them does not authorize submission.

When a retrieval check is requested or terminology is uncertain and research tools are available, try plausible combinations of generic candidate terms in an academic search service and inspect the topics of returned papers. Check whether they concern the intended phenomenon, method, and audience. Narrow an overly broad term or replace an ambiguous one based on what was actually found. Search only what is needed to check terminology; unpublished passages or exact unpublished results are not needed for this task. Report which terms were checked and any access limits. A relevance check is not a novelty search or a guarantee of indexing or ranking. If browsing is unavailable or prohibited, select from the supplied paper and label retrieval performance untested.

## 5. Check the three components together

For a combined task, identifying a few central concepts can help develop the title and then the abstract; revisiting them after drafting is equally valid. Use the same technical terms for the same entities. Do not force every keyword into the title or sacrifice the abstract's logic to fit a predetermined sequence.

Temporarily hide the manuscript and read the title, abstract, and keyword list together. Can a colleague identify the subject, question, approach, main answer, and its scope? Then reopen the paper and check that all three represent it faithfully. A descriptive title need not announce a result, but a result-led title must be supported by the abstract and body. Remove keyword-only topics, repair mismatched populations or conditions, and avoid a broader claim in the title than in the abstract.

If a joint edit reveals an inconsistency, fix it within the authorized revision depth or flag it separately for a language-only task. Deliver only the requested components and consequential notes.
