# Grammar decisions from the academic-writing slides

Read the relevant section for detailed correction, a disputed construction, or a teaching request. This expands the supplied PPT's rules 1–8; it is not a compulsory checklist for every edit. Preserve the editing depth and scientific invariants in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). Examples here are invented teaching sentences, not research evidence. Source locations and qualified historical advice are in [source-map.md](source-map.md).

Contents: possessives; lists; parenthetical and restrictive information; clause boundaries and conjunctions; fragments; modifier attachment; variable mappings; line-end division; final recheck.

## 1. Possessives: identify the possessor before adding punctuation

Distinguish a singular possessor, a plural possessor, and an attributive noun. A normal singular possessive takes **'s**, including names ending in s: `the device's response`, `Jones's model`. A regular plural ending in s takes a final apostrophe: `the devices' responses`. Do not convert an ordinary plural or a technical label into a possessive. Keep `its` distinct from `it's`.

The PPT also presents classical/biblical names such as `Jesus'` and `Achilles'`, and expressions such as `for conscience' sake`, as historical exceptions. Those spellings are convention-dependent; do not extend them to all names ending in s or overwrite a specified house style. An **of** construction can remove an awkward possessive without changing the owner: `the specimen's surface` and `the surface of the specimen` express the same ownership relationship. Choose by clarity, not a ban on possessives with inanimate nouns. Preserve proper names and reference metadata.

## 2. Lists and the serial comma

For three or more coordinate items, use a consistent list convention. A comma before the final and/or often makes item boundaries clear. Check the actual grouping: `the controls, sample A and sample B` could name three items or describe the controls. If grouping affects the experiment, determine the intended meaning rather than inserting punctuation by habit.

Respect official organization names and quoted titles; a house-style preference does not authorize respelling them. For items containing internal commas, semicolons or an explicit list may clarify the boundaries. Preserve which condition belongs to each sample.

`Etc.` denotes further items of the same kind. In a conventional inline list it is preceded by a comma; in mid-sentence, a following comma may close the list: `Buffers, salts, etc., were screened.` Prefer an explicit list when completeness is scientifically necessary, and flag unspecified items rather than inventing them. Avoid combining `such as` with a redundant `etc.`. The abbreviation's period is retained; do not add a second period at the end of the sentence. Citation and journal conventions still govern punctuation placement.

## 3. Parenthetical material and relative clauses

First decide whether the material identifies the entity or supplies additional information. Removing text while leaving a grammatical sentence is only a clue: removal may still change the population or conditions.

- A mid-sentence parenthesis normally needs both boundaries: `The estimate, however, remained uncertain.` Do not retain only one of its paired commas. At the start or end, the sentence boundary supplies the other edge.
- Weakly interrupting adverbs can be integrated: `The estimate therefore remains provisional.` Do not automatically surround every occurrence of `therefore` or `however` with identical punctuation.
- Restrictive information selects a subset: `The specimens that fractured were excluded.` Nonrestrictive information adds a statement about an already identified set: `The specimens, which fractured, were excluded.` Do not exchange these readings without evidence. Relative words alone do not settle the punctuation; the intended restriction matters.
- When a conjunction precedes an interruption, distinguish the clause boundary from the interruption: `The fit improved, but, as expected, the residuals remained correlated.` Recast an awkward cluster when possible: `The fit improved, but the residuals remained correlated, as expected.` Here `as expected` must be supported, not an editorial addition. Do not apply a blanket rule to move every comma before a conjunction.

For scientific relative-clause examples, see [worked examples, example 9](worked-examples.md#9-punctuation-changes-the-sample-population).

## 4. Clause boundaries: diagnose before choosing a repair

Locate the subject and finite predicate on each side of a proposed boundary. A participle such as `showing` is not by itself a finite predicate. A subordinate clause beginning with because/although/if cannot normally stand alone in continuous expository prose, even when it contains a subject and verb.

| Relationship at the boundary | Usual treatment | Teaching example |
|---|---|---|
| Two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction | Comma before and, but, for, or, nor, so, or yet | `The sensor saturated, so we reduced the gain.` |
| Two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction | Semicolon | `The sensor saturated; the reference channel remained within range.` |
| Two independent assertions that merit separate sentences | Period | `The sensor saturated. We excluded that trace.` |
| Independent clauses linked by a conjunctive adverb | Semicolon or period before the second clause; punctuate the adverb by position | `The sensor saturated; therefore, we reduced the gain.` |
| One subject with coordinated predicates | Usually no comma between predicates | `We reduced the gain and repeated the scan.` |
| Coordinated words or phrases | Do not apply the independent-clause comma rule | `The film was thin but stable.` |
| An introductory dependent clause followed by a main clause | Usually a comma after the dependent clause | `Although the film was thin, it remained stable.` |

A comma alone usually cannot join two independent clauses. Choose among a semicolon, a period, or comma plus an appropriate coordinating conjunction. These repairs are not interchangeable in scientific meaning: `but` asserts contrast; `so` or `therefore` expresses a consequence. If only coexistence is known, do not manufacture causality while fixing punctuation. Very short, closely linked clauses sometimes omit a comma under a house style; do not turn an optional readability edit into a scientific error.

### Conjunctive adverbs are not coordinating conjunctions

`Therefore`, `thus`, `however`, `moreover`, `accordingly`, and `nevertheless` do not license a comma splice. Compare:

- Incorrect boundary: `The detector saturated, therefore the trace was excluded.`
- Boundary repaired: `The detector saturated; therefore, the trace was excluded.`
- Also possible: `The detector saturated. The trace was therefore excluded.`
- Coordinating conjunction: `The detector saturated, so the trace was excluded.`

These examples assume exclusion was a consequence of saturation. If that relation is unknown, preserve separate observations or query it. Do not replace a legitimate `so` merely to make the prose sound formal. The PPT's older advice to avoid it conflicts with its later scientific example; adopt the distinction between **so as a conjunction** and **therefore as an adverb**. This is also supported by [Purdue OWL's clause-punctuation guidance](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/commas/commas_vs_semicolons.html).

### Express the relation and preserve rhythm

Repeated `A, and B` structures can obscure whether B is a reason, qualification, consequence, or parallel observation. Where supported, make the relation explicit with because, although, if, or another precise construction. A prepositional phrase can compress an established circumstance: `Because the records were incomplete, the estimate remained uncertain` can become `With incomplete records, the estimate remained uncertain` only if the weaker circumstantial wording preserves the intended argument. `Owing to` explicitly retains a causal assertion. Check `as` and `since` for temporal ambiguity.

Do not force every sentence into a compact subordinate-clause pattern. Both gradually unfolding and delayed-main-point sentences can be effective. Choose the order that makes the evidence and qualification easiest to follow; vary structure because the thought changes.

The PPT permits comma-only coordination in short, highly balanced literary or proverbial constructions. Recognize it when preserving a quotation or a deliberate rhetorical form. Do not use that narrow exception as a general repair for scientific prose. Equations and their variable definitions require a separate mapping check below, not automatic comma-splice rejection.

## 5. Fragments: restore the missing grammatical relationship

In running prose, inspect material after a period that starts with an -ing/-ed phrase, a dependent clause, or an appositive noun phrase. Repair a fragment by reattaching it to the sentence it belongs to or by supplying an independently supported subject and finite predicate.

| Source | Possible repair | Meaning check |
|---|---|---|
| `We measured displacement. Using a laser sensor.` | `We measured displacement using a laser sensor.` | The sensor belongs to this measurement |
| `We used model R. A linear approximation valid below 1% strain.` | `We used model R, a linear approximation valid below 1% strain.` | Keep the validity limit; do not imply broader applicability |
| `The specimen was excluded. Because its identifier was missing.` | `The specimen was excluded because its identifier was missing.` | The stated exclusion reason is preserved |

A missing relation may be substantive; ask locally rather than inventing a subject, result, or explanation. A heading, panel label, table entry, or conventional caption phrase need not be a full sentence. The PPT's intentional fragments for emphasis are stylistic exceptions; recognize purposeful forms without manufacturing dramatic fragments throughout a research article.

## 6. Opening modifiers: find the actor or recipient again after editing

For a participial opening, identify who performs the action (often -ing) or what receives it (often -ed), then compare with the main-clause subject. Check meaning rather than suffix alone. `Heated to 60 °C, the specimen expanded` has a plausible recipient; `Using high-speed imaging, crack growth was tracked` makes crack growth appear to use the instrument.

Choose a repair that preserves what is known:

1. Use the known actor as subject: `Using high-speed imaging, we tracked crack growth` when the authors actually did so.
2. Give the introductory action its own explicit subject in a clause: `When the team used high-speed imaging, crack growth could be tracked` only if the team and this capability claim are supplied.
3. Recast the instrument or circumstance directly: `Crack growth was tracked with high-speed imaging.` This avoids inventing an actor.

Purpose infinitives deserve an attachment check too, but do not mechanically ban every purpose phrase with passive voice. `To estimate diffusivity, we fitted the release curve` is clear when the authors are known; `The release curve was fitted to estimate diffusivity` can retain the procedure as topic. Established impersonal expressions are not automatically errors. Explain a real ambiguity rather than labelling every surface pattern a dangling modifier.

After a repair, reread the actor, recipient, method, purpose, and result. Merely moving an opening phrase to the end may leave it ambiguous. Never use a smoother sentence as evidence that attachment is correct.

## 7. Equations, definitions, and respectively

Both explicit sequential definitions and `respectively` mappings can be clear:

- `Here, a denotes the signal, b denotes the background, and c denotes the correction.`
- `Here, a, b, and c denote the signal, background, and correction, respectively.`

Before converting, compare the number, order, and identity of symbols and definitions. Retain subscripts, units, signs, and the equation itself. Prefer explicit repeated definitions or a table when items are long or differently qualified. Do not remove `respectively` as needless wording if it carries the mapping, and do not reverse list order independently for stylistic variety. Do not diagnose every coordinated definition after `where` as a comma splice.

## 8. Word division is a typesetting operation

The PPT distinguishes three historical heuristics: divide at morphological boundaries; consider pronunciation/syllables (sometimes after a vowel); and split suitable doubled consonants, while retaining consonants belonging together at a stem ending. It also warns against stranded single letters or tiny terminal fragments. These explain the original book's examples, not an algorithm that works for every modern technical word. Check a dictionary and the target language/style for a disputed division; do not infer a word's etymology from a plausible-looking split.

The slide examples include `atmo-sphere`, `ordi-nary`, and `refer-ring`, with `tell-ing` illustrating preservation of a stem-final pair. Treat these as demonstrations of the historical heuristics, not instructions to insert those hyphens into running text or to derive a universal vowel/double-letter rule.

During drafting and language editing, retain complete words. Do not insert literal hyphens or hard line breaks to imitate slide wrapping. Preserve genuine compound hyphens, minus signs, gene names, chemical strings, DOI/URL text, and identifiers.

For an actual typesetting request, use the layout engine's language-specific hyphenation first. TeX uses patterns and exceptions, not only a dictionary of recognized whole words; see the [LaTeX configuration guide](https://www.latex-project.org/help/documentation/cfgguide.pdf). The PPT's final slide simplifies this distinction. Inspect the affected line and rendered page: an overfull box can also arise from a formula, URL, unbreakable material, or width settings, so it does not prove that a word is missing from a dictionary. If a discretionary break or exception is needed, verify the word's allowed division and keep that intervention in the typesetting source. Do not repair a layout warning by silently changing scientific content.

## 9. Recheck the scientific statement

For the edited passage, verify the same actor, population, order of mappings, comparison, evidence, conditions, and certainty. Separate a required grammar repair from an optional house-style choice. Give a brief before/after explanation for a teaching request; deliver only the necessary text for ordinary polishing. A request to explain a rule does not authorize rewriting unrelated sections.
