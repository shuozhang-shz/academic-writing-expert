# Worked examples

All scientific examples below are invented teaching fixtures, not published findings or reusable evidence. The aim is to clarify editorial decisions. Do not copy their numbers or conclusions into real work.

For additional examples of clause boundaries, conjunctions, sentence fragments, modifier repairs, and equation-variable definitions, use the relevant section of [grammar and punctuation](grammar-and-punctuation.md).

## 1. Concision without losing the experiment

**Source:** In order to determine the effect of temperature on the response time, measurements of the response time were carried out at 20, 30, and 40 °C, with five independent devices measured at each temperature.

**Revision:** Response time was measured at 20, 30, and 40 °C using five independent devices at each temperature to determine the effect of temperature.

**Why:** Removes an empty noun–verb construction and repetition while retaining the temperatures, endpoint, and independent sample unit. A passive voice is appropriate. The sentence makes no new claim about what was found.

## 2. A language-only request and an overclaim

**Request:** Correct grammar only; keep the conclusion.

**Source:** The observational survey prove that longer sleep causes better examination performance.

**Faithful language edit:** The observational survey proves that longer sleep causes better examination performance.

**Separate scientific note:** The grammatical edit preserves a causal claim that the supplied description does not establish. If only an association was measured, an evidence-calibrated alternative would be: 'The survey found an association between longer sleep and better examination performance.'

**Why:** The scope of the edit and the scientific concern are both visible. Do not silently change 'causes' to 'is associated with' in a grammar-only revision, and do not present the unchanged claim as scientifically verified.

## 3. A null result is not equivalence

**Source:** Treatment did not significantly change mass (p = 0.18).

**Conservative revision:** The change in mass was not statistically significant (p = 0.18).

**Author query if a scientific interpretation is needed:** What were the effect estimate, uncertainty, test, and comparison? The reported p value alone does not show that treatment has no effect or that groups are equivalent.

**Avoid:** 'Treatment had no effect on mass.'

## 4. Chinese-to-English without inventing statistics

**Source:** 与对照样品相比，样品 A 在 25 °C、10 mA cm⁻² 下的过电位降低了 20 mV，说明其在这一条件下所需过电位更低。

**Revision:** At 25 °C and 10 mA cm⁻², sample A required an overpotential 20 mV lower than that of the control.

**Why:** Preserves the sample, comparator, conditions, quantity, and direction. Removes a duplicated interpretation. Does not add significance, a mechanism, or an assertion of general superiority.

## 5. A qualifier that must survive shortening

**Source:** The signal was not detected under the tested conditions, although the detection limit does not exclude lower concentrations.

**Revision:** The signal was undetected under the tested conditions; concentrations below the detection limit remain possible.

**Avoid:** 'The analyte was absent.' The scope and measurement limit are part of the finding.

## 6. Parallel comparison and numerical mapping

**Source:** Sample A had higher conductivity than sample B's strength. A and B had conductivities of 8 and 5 S m⁻¹, respectively.

**Action:** Flag the conductivity/strength comparison as scientifically unclear. The numerical statement can be retained or revised to 'The conductivities of A and B were 8 and 5 S m⁻¹, respectively.'

**Why:** 'Respectively' carries a useful mapping. A line editor cannot assume the intended comparator in the first sentence.

## 7. Paragraph unity from supplied facts

**Given facts:** A coating was examined at 10 and 20 °C. The reported median release times were 12 and 7 min, respectively. No diffusion measurement was supplied. The manuscript asks whether the release time changes with temperature.

**Supported Results draft:** Median release time was shorter at 20 °C than at 10 °C. The reported medians were 7 and 12 min, respectively. These observations describe a temperature-dependent difference under the tested conditions.

**Discussion boundary:** Faster diffusion is a possible explanation to investigate, not a mechanism established by these data. Sample counts, uncertainty, and inferential analysis are not supplied; do not invent them. If the claim must be inferential rather than descriptive, request that information.

**Why:** Builds a paragraph around one result without turning a plausible mechanism into evidence.

## 8. Tense follows purpose

**Revision:** 'Figure 2 shows the temperature dependence. We measured the response at three temperatures.'

**Why:** Present tense describes the available figure; past tense describes completed measurements. Consistency of rhetorical purpose does not require one tense across both sentences.

## 9. Punctuation changes the sample population

**Source:** The samples which were heated showed cracking.

**Decision:** Determine whether only heated samples cracked or whether all samples were heated and then cracked. 'The samples that were heated showed cracking' preserves a restrictive reading. 'The samples, which were heated, showed cracking' presents heating as additional information about all referenced samples. Do not add commas until the intended scope is clear.

## 10. Evidence outranks impressive wording

**Request:** Make the result sound groundbreaking.

**Evidence:** One algorithm exceeded one baseline on one supplied dataset; no other comparisons were provided.

**Action:** Describe the actual metric and comparison precisely if supplied. Identify the concrete contribution in the approach if known. Do not assert universal superiority, world records, broad generalization, or unprecedented novelty. Missing comparison data are not a rhetorical problem that adjectives can solve.
