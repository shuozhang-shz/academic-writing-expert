# Workflow: drafting, revision, translation

Read the part relevant to the task. The phases are editorial priorities, not a mandatory procession of approvals or visible reports.

## Establish the writing brief

Extract what is already known: manuscript genre and section; discipline and audience; central question; evidence supplied; target length; journal if specified; edit depth; English variety; output format. Match an existing consistent English variety; for a new draft use one consistent variety without delaying work. A missing journal name does not block a general scientific draft.

Distinguish these starting states:

- **Notes or figures:** extract findings and conditions; build a supported narrative, then draft. Do not make unreported measurements out of visual impressions. Ask for source numbers if precise reporting depends on them.
- **A complete rough draft:** inspect its argument and preserve a source copy; identify the few problems that materially obstruct the reader, then revise within scope.
- **A short passage:** use nearby context only as needed to resolve terms, references, or transitions. Do not search the full project by default.
- **A concept without results:** provide an argument, proposed outline, or clearly labeled conceptual draft. Separate proposed experiments from completed work. A full empirical paper cannot responsibly acquire a fabricated Results section.

Ask a focused question when alternative interpretations would change the science. Otherwise state a reasonable working assumption briefly and proceed. For an unresolved local value or meaning, use a descriptive author query such as `[AUTHOR QUERY: specify whether n counts participants or technical replicates]` in an annotated draft. Keep such notes outside clean prose when possible. Never replace missing values with plausible numbers.

## Develop and test an argument

Identify the question, main answer, reasons it matters to this audience, evidence for the answer, and its limits. Write these as a compact brief; scale its formality to the task. The supplied material may support several distinct stories. Offer alternatives only when selecting one would change the manuscript, and show the evidence and tradeoff behind each.

Organize by the reader's questions rather than laboratory chronology. A Results sequence might establish the phenomenon, test its robustness, examine an explanation, and evaluate its scope; many papers need a different sequence. Every figure and paragraph should perform a recognizable job.

Use drafting to find weaknesses. A rapid paragraph can expose an undefined quantity, an unsupported comparison, or a missing inference. Repair the reasoning, remove the unsupported step, or label the hypothesis before spending effort on elegant phrasing. This combines the two thinking essays: writing can develop thought, and critical evaluation of a draft can also develop thought.

For a substantial claim, consider the strongest plausible alternative explanation and what the supplied evidence actually distinguishes. Do not manufacture a counterargument ritual for every sentence or invent a limitation absent from the study. Distinguish a known limitation, an unresolved possibility, and an experiment proposed to test it.

## Draft in an evidence-dependent order

When helpful, start with the results and the methods needed to interpret them, then build the introduction and discussion around what was actually established. Write or revisit title and abstract after the central contribution is stable. This is an option, not a fixed order; follow the user's requested section and genre.

Turn evidence into paragraphs with a purpose and a discernible logical relationship. Keep observations, interpretation, and speculation distinguishable. Preserve citations on the claims they support when moving sentences. Do not expand a narrow observation into a broad application merely to finish an attractive story.

## Choose revision depth deliberately

| Level | Appropriate changes | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Copy-edit | Grammar, spelling, punctuation, articles, local clarity | No scientific or structural reinterpretation |
| Line edit / default polish | Sentence flow, redundancy, modifier placement, nearby cohesion | Preserve claim strength, content, and overall argument |
| Substantive revision | Paragraph order, explicit reasoning, calibrated conclusions, section organization | Explain changed implications; preserve the evidentiary record |
| Reconstruction | New outline and prose from supplied evidence | Identify newly proposed interpretations; retain traceability |

A stronger-sounding sentence is not necessarily a better one. When the original overclaims but only language editing is authorized, provide a faithful language edit and separate scientific concern; do not silently endorse or silently repair the claim. If the user says to correct scientific overclaims, that authorization permits an evidence-calibrated rewrite without asking again.

## Chinese-to-English scientific rewriting

Recover the proposition before translating the words: who or what did what, relative to which comparator, under which conditions, with what evidence and certainty? Keep a small term mapping for repeated technical concepts. Do not infer an English acronym expansion from its letters alone.

Rebuild topic-comment chains, stacked noun phrases, and missing subjects into natural English. Preserve legitimate ambiguity until context resolves it. Render 实现, 显著, 证明, 规律, 性能, 效率, and 机制 by their scientific meaning; none has a single automatic translation. For example, 显著 may mean statistically significant, practically large, or simply noticeable. Check the evidence before choosing.

A short semantic back-check is useful: does the English still preserve the Chinese statement's agent, comparison, direction, scope, and uncertainty? Report only consequential translation choices unless the user is learning writing and requests explanation.

## Long manuscripts and interrupted work

For work spanning many sections or sessions, maintain a compact working dossier in the authorized workspace when useful. Include the current brief, source locations, claim-to-evidence mapping, terminology decisions, locked numbers and definitions, unresolved author questions, and section status. The [dossier template](../assets/manuscript-dossier.md) is optional; remove unused fields.

Read the current manuscript and dossier before continuing after interruption. Recheck a changed central claim wherever it appears in the title, abstract, discussion, captions, and conclusion. Do not declare a whole manuscript checked after reading only one section.

Support intellectual ownership through concrete, reviewable decisions: point out an ambiguous inference, distinguish two possible interpretations, and describe what evidence would settle them. A Chinese note, oral transcript, or mixed-language outline is a valid starting point. Do not require the researcher to write an English first draft as proof of engagement.
